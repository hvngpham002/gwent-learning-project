# Critical Literature Sweep for a Gwent-Style Collectible Card Game AI Research Program

**Date prepared:** 8 May 2026
**Scope:** Imperfect-information game AI, CCG/TCG environments, action masking, self-play RL, MCTS/ISMCTS, CFR family, opponent modeling, evaluation methodology, human-likeness/difficulty control.
**Posture:** Critical. Many "general self-play" results from perfect-information games (AlphaZero/MuZero) are flagged as **background-only** with respect to a Gwent-style imperfect-information game; CFR-family and ISMCTS-family methods are positioned as the technically defensible near-term cores. No citations have been invented; where I could not locate a primary source via search within the budget I have flagged the entry as `needs-verification` or `secondary-lead-only`.

## Top-10 papers — short notes

### 1. Cowling, Powley, Whitehouse (2012) — Information Set Monte Carlo Tree Search [#1]

This is the reference baseline for any planner sitting on top of a Gwent engine. ISMCTS searches over information sets rather than ground game states, with three variants (SO-, MO-, MT-ISMCTS) covering varying levels of opponent-information modeling. The paper shows clear gains over Perfect-Information Monte Carlo (PIMC) on Dou Di Zhu, LOTR Confrontation, and Phantom 4×4×4. For Gwent, MO-ISMCTS is the natural fit because both seats have hidden hands and deck order.

Caveats to flag for the team: ISMCTS still suffers from strategy fusion at the root and from information leakage into the opponent subtree, partly fixed by the re-determinizing variant [#4]. ISMCTS is **not** equilibrium-targeting; against an optimizing opponent it is exploitable. This paper should anchor the legal-heuristic-v1 → ISMCTS-v1 → Deep-CFR/ReBeL roadmap, with [#3] supplying the diagnostic for whether ISMCTS will work well in Gwent (leaf correlation, bias, disambiguation). Implementation risk is low: open-source ISMCTS implementations exist; the engine already exposes the per-row safe legal-action API the algorithm needs.

### 2. Long, Sturtevant, Buro, Furtak (2010) — Understanding the Success of PIMC [#3]

This paper is the most cost-effective single piece of literature for the Gwent project: it tells you, before committing engineering effort, *whether* determinization-based search (PIMC and by extension ISMCTS) will work well on a given imperfect-information game. The three measurable properties are leaf correlation (do nearby leaves share payoffs), bias (is one side advantaged regardless), and disambiguation factor (how quickly hidden info is revealed). Trick-taking card games tend to score favourably; Gwent's three-round structure with intermediate scoring and round-passing as a strategic resource is plausible but unproven on these axes.

Concrete recommendation: Cluster F's first ISMCTS spike should include a measurement run that estimates these three properties from logs of legal-heuristic-v0 self-play. If leaf correlation is low and disambiguation is slow (as is plausible for Gwent because the deck remains hidden across rounds), the team should not over-commit to ISMCTS and should instead prepare for a CFR-family or ReBeL-style approach earlier than planned. This paper is methodological, low risk, and highly relevant.

### 3. Heinrich, Silver (2016) — NFSP [#8]

NFSP is the cleanest deep-learning baseline that *targets* approximate Nash equilibrium in 2p0s imperfect-information games. The agent learns two networks: a best-response Q-network and an average-policy classifier, mixed via anticipatory dynamics. On Leduc poker NFSP approached Nash; common deep RL methods diverged. On Limit Texas Hold'em it approached state-of-the-art with no domain knowledge.

For Gwent, NFSP is attractive as a "legal-heuristic-v1 + ML" baseline because (a) it does not require search at training time, (b) it scales to neural function approximation over the per-row legal-action representation, and (c) it produces an exploitability proxy via best-response training. Risks: NFSP is slow to converge in larger games and is dominated by Deep CFR [#7] in many settings. The team should treat NFSP as the *baseline* against which Deep CFR or ReBeL improvements are measured, not the final planner. Critically, the paper *does* provide theory backing for the convergence claim, satisfying the project's "no assuming self-play converges" constraint.

### 4. Brown, Lerer, Gross, Sandholm (2019) — Deep CFR [#7]

Deep CFR is the most practical neural CFR variant and is the strongest candidate for the medium-term Gwent training pipeline. Instead of tabular regret tables it learns regret networks via outcome sampling and trains a policy network from the average strategy. The paper shows it beats prior NFSP results and approaches CFR+ in HUNL subgames at a fraction of memory.

For a Gwent-style game with small decks and limited rounds, the information-set count is large (state ≈ public history × per-seat hand × per-seat deck-permutation possibilities) but bounded enough that Deep CFR is plausible if proper public-private decomposition and sampling are used. Risks: high variance in regret estimation, careful sampling needed (external vs. outcome), and the policy network must accept variable legal-action sets — pair this with the action-masking practice from [#25] and possibly action embeddings from [#26] only if needed. Deep CFR has explicit imperfect-information evaluation (exploitability is reported) and therefore *does not* trip the project's "no perfect-info-only methods" red flag.

### 5. Brown, Bakhtin, Lerer, Gong (2020) — ReBeL [#13]

ReBeL is the closest published thing to "AlphaZero for imperfect-information games" with theoretical convergence guarantees in 2p0s settings. It expands "state" to public belief states (joint belief distributions over players' private info given common knowledge) and runs gradient-ascent-like search over them, with value/policy networks trained via self-play. It produced superhuman HUNL play with far less domain knowledge than prior poker AIs and converges to Nash in tabular settings.

For Gwent, ReBeL is the *aspirational* planner: the per-row safe observation already exposes the public-history axis ReBeL needs, and the per-seat private state (hand + deck order) is the belief axis. Implementation risk is high — Meta's open-source release is non-trivial, search over belief states is engineering-heavy, and the value network must be trained on (PBS, value) pairs generated by the search itself. The *value* of citing ReBeL early in the program is to anchor the long-term roadmap; *do not* attempt ReBeL before Deep CFR baseline and ISMCTS-v1 are operational. Player of Games / Student of Games [#20] is an evolution of the same lineage and should be cited alongside.

### 6. Schmid et al. (2021/2023) — Player of Games / Student of Games [#20]

Student of Games (the peer-reviewed Science Advances version of the earlier "Player of Games" arXiv release) is the most general-purpose successor to ReBeL and arguably the best-publicized description of how to combine guided search + self-play + game-theoretic reasoning across both perfect and imperfect information. It uses Growing-Tree CFR (GT-CFR) plus Counterfactual Value-and-Policy Networks (CVPNs). Empirically it reaches strong play in chess and Go, beats Slumbot in HUNL, and beats the prior SOTA in Scotland Yard.

For the Gwent program, Student of Games is the strongest candidate for a unified long-term planner: a single architecture covers the perfect-info aspects (public state, three-round score, leader use) and the imperfect-info aspects (hand, deck order). Engineering complexity is significant: query buffers, recursive sub-solves, distributed actor/trainer split, and CFR within the search loop. Citing this paper in the roadmap doc lets the team avoid inventing a "novel" hybrid; it documents an end-to-end architecture that has actually been validated on a hidden-info board game (Scotland Yard) that is structurally closer to Gwent than poker is.

### 7. Pérolat et al. (2022) — DeepNash / R-NaD [#14]

DeepNash is important for the Gwent program *as a contrarian data point*: it shows that a model-free RL algorithm with the right multi-agent dynamics (Regularized Nash Dynamics) can find an ε-Nash policy in a very large 2p0s imperfect-information game (Stratego) **without search**. The paper reports top-3 ranking on Gravon's human ladder. The key technical contributions are: (a) recurrent encoder over private observation history; (b) regularized FoReL/replicator dynamics that converge instead of cycling; (c) low-prob-action zeroing at play time to avoid pathological mistakes.

Two cautions: (1) compute is industrial-scale — DeepMind report extensive distributed training; this is *not* a small-academic-compute method. (2) The result is on Stratego where the imperfect-information arises mostly from the deployment phase rather than from a continuously hidden hand; whether R-NaD transfers to a card game with continuously refreshed hidden information has not, to my knowledge, been independently verified. For Gwent, R-NaD is best treated as a long-term option — a single-network policy without search would be very attractive in production — but only after Deep CFR / ReBeL-style baselines establish that the equilibrium target is reachable.

### 8. Bakhtin et al. (2023) — Diplodocus / RL-DiL-piKL, with Jacob et al. (2022) — piKL [#15, #16]

These are the cleanest published treatments of "play strongly **and** like a human / at a controllable strength." piKL adds a KL regularization toward a behavioral-cloning policy of human play to a regret-minimizing search; the regularization coefficient λ trades off strength vs. human-likeness while preserving move-prediction accuracy. Diplodocus extends this into self-play RL in no-press Diplomacy, and outperformed all but one human in a 200-game tournament.

Direct relevance to Gwent's "difficulty scaling for human-like opponents" constraint: piKL is materially better than the obvious alternative (a strong engine + softmax-temperature blunder) because (1) it preserves human-like *style* not just human-like *strength*, and (2) it has theoretical justification (no-regret under modified utility). The cost is that Gwent needs human play data to do this properly. A near-term hybrid — Maia-style [#36] tier-conditioned BC for the difficulty levels, then piKL as a search-time regularizer once a search planner exists — is likely the right roadmap. Pairing this with Holmgård et al. procedural personas [#39] gives an alternative when human data is scarce.

### 9. Huang, Ontañón (2020) — Invalid Action Masking [#25]

This is the small but highly load-bearing paper for the Cluster F observation/action/reward design. It (a) provides theoretical justification that masking invalid actions in policy-gradient algorithms produces a valid gradient, (b) shows empirically that performance gain over alternatives (penalty, replacement) grows with the size of the invalid-action set, and (c) compares masking regimes (training-only, inference-only, both) — finding that consistent masking dominates.

Gwent's engine already exposes ordered legal action lists per row, with a variable action set per turn (mulligan vs. card play vs. leader use vs. prompt response vs. pass). The implication is concrete: the ML policy network must consume the legal-action list as part of input or apply a logit-level mask conditioned on the current legal set. Do **not** train without masking. If the action set ever grows beyond the practical limit of one-hot encoding (large prompt-response trees), then turn to action embeddings / Wolpertinger-style policies [#26], but only as a fallback. This paper is a near-zero-risk citation that directly validates the engineering plan.

### 10. Cowling, Ward, Powley (2012) — Ensemble Determinization for Magic: The Gathering [#2]

The most directly comparable published benchmark to Gwent's structural class: a true CCG with hidden hand and shuffled deck, evaluated with determinization-based MCTS. The authors examine multiple determinizations per move and pruning strategies to mitigate strategy fusion, decompose moves into binary yes/no decision trees for finer-grained search, and show significant gains over basic MCTS on fixed decks.

Two implications for Gwent. First, determinized MCTS is a reasonable starting point that will substantially beat the legal-heuristic-v0 baseline; the engineering already lines up with Gwent's per-seat safe observation API. Second, the paper itself surfaces strategy fusion as a known limitation, motivating the move from ISMCTS [#1] toward re-determinizing ISMCTS [#4] or CFR-family methods [#5–#7, #13, #20] as the program matures. Note: the M:TG work uses fixed decks and does not model deck-conditioning or arena-style drafting — that gap is filled by the LOCM line [#31, #32, #33].

## Critical Synthesis & Implications for the Gwent Program

**The literature does *not* support a clean "AlphaZero for Gwent" approach.** Every primary source that has actually evaluated AlphaZero/MuZero-style algorithms on imperfect-information settings (DeepStack, ReBeL, Player/Student of Games) has had to bolt on a public-belief-state machinery + CFR-style equilibrium reasoning + value/policy networks tied to that belief state. Papers using AlphaZero/MuZero in their pure forms (#21, #22) are explicitly perfect-information; using them on Gwent without a belief-state overlay would amount to *cheating with full state* during search — exactly the antipattern the project flags. Treat MuZero/AlphaZero as background-only.

**The literature *does* clearly support a CFR-family + ISMCTS-family stack as the technical core.** The combination of (i) ISMCTS / re-determinizing ISMCTS [#1, #4] for the heuristic-v1 phase, (ii) ensemble determinization analysis [#2] and PIMC bias diagnosis [#3] to predict whether the simpler approaches will work, (iii) Deep CFR [#7] / NFSP [#8] for the equilibrium-targeting ML phase, and (iv) ReBeL [#13] / Student of Games [#20] as the long-term unified planner, is the dominant published trajectory in 2p0s imperfect-info card-game-class problems. CFR+ [#19] should be kept as the small-scale "ground truth" solver during prototyping.

**For human-like difficulty, behavioral cloning + KL-regularized search is the dominant published recipe.** Maia [#36] provides the per-skill-tier BC pattern; piKL / Diplodocus [#15, #16] provide the search-time regularization; Holmgård et al. [#39] provides the no-human-data fallback via procedural personas. Avoid the "softmax temperature blunder" approach that the project document already correctly identifies as drunk-pathological.

**Action masking is settled engineering [#25]; action embeddings [#26] should be a fallback, not a default.** The engine's existing per-row legal-action export is already the right interface.

**Hearthstone and LOCM are the two viable structural reference benchmarks.** Hearthstone's published work [#29, #30] gives a SabberStone-style legal-action API and partial-observation game state. LOCM [#31, #32, #33] is closer to a research-friendly small CCG with deck drafting. Both are imperfect comparators because Gwent has three rounds and pass-as-strategic-resource, but they are the closest published prior art and a good baseline for matchup matrices.

**Self-play convergence claims must be cited carefully.** AlphaStar [#23] and OpenAI Five [#24] are explicitly not equilibrium-targeting; their league/PFSP and PPO-at-scale results do *not* prove convergence in 2p0s imperfect-info games. NFSP [#8], Deep CFR [#7], R-NaD/DeepNash [#14], and ReBeL/SoG [#13, #20] are the only verified primary sources where self-play in an imperfect-information setting comes with theoretical or empirical convergence/exploitability evidence. The project document's constraint here is correctly imposed by the literature.

**Recommended near-term Cluster F implementation citations:**
- Legal-heuristic-v1 design: [#1, #2, #3, #29].
- Search/self-play planner choice: ISMCTS first [#1, #4], then Deep CFR [#7] / NFSP [#8] for the ML baseline, with ReBeL [#13] / Student of Games [#20] as the long-term roadmap target.
- ML obs/action/reward design: [#25] for action masking; [#23] for variable-action-set architecture template (with the caveat that AlphaStar is RTS, not card-game); [#26] only as fallback.
- Evaluation ladder: Elo/Glicko/TrueSkill standard practice (no specific peer-reviewed primary needs to be invented; Bowling et al. [#19] uses exploitability and milli-big-blinds-per-game as the gold standard); matchup matrices and PFSP [#23]; approximate exploitability via best-response training [#34].
- Difficulty scaling: [#36] Maia (BC by skill tier), [#16] piKL (KL-regularized search), [#39] procedural personas.
- Deck-conditioned vs faction-specialist: [#32, #33] for self-play RL on drafts and battles in LOCM; [#35] for the taxonomic framing.

## Caveats and Verification Notes

1. **Browne et al. and Świechowski et al. surveys [#27, #28]** are useful for citation discovery but should not themselves be cited as primary sources for any method.
2. **The "Hostetler/PIMC card game" anchor in the original brief was not located as a discrete primary paper.** The verified canonical PIMC-bias citation is Long, Sturtevant, Buro, Furtak (AAAI 2010) [#3]. Hostetler et al.'s primary papers concern RTS strategy inference [#37, #38], not card-game PIMC. Treat any "Hostetler card-game PIMC" reference as `needs-verification`.
3. **A "Ward et al. Hearthstone" environment paper was not confirmed as a discrete primary source.** Use the Dockhorn/Mostaghim CIG paper [#30] and the SabberStone framework as the anchor instead.
4. **Compute footprints for DeepNash [#14], AlphaStar [#23], OpenAI Five [#24], Cicero [#17], Player/Student of Games [#20], and Pluribus/Libratus [#11, #12]** are large. Treat these as architectural references, not as small-academic-compute training plans.
5. **Self-play for imperfect-info games with theory backing** is presently NFSP, MCCFR/CFR/CFR+/Deep CFR, ReBeL/Student of Games, R-NaD/DeepNash, and PSRO/Pipeline-PSRO — anything outside this list cited as "self-play converges" should be challenged.
6. **Cicero [#17] and Diplomacy work** is multiplayer and dialogue-heavy; the Diplodocus paper [#15] is the directly relevant 2p0s/no-press analogue for human-like search and is the better citation for the Gwent context.
7. **All entries above are publicly searchable and were verified via publisher abstracts, arXiv pages, or proceedings entries during the sweep**, except where flagged `likely-primary-source`, `needs-verification`, or `secondary-lead-only`. No citations were invented; uncertain entries are explicitly flagged for the team to verify before inclusion in any external document.
