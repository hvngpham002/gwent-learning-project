# **AI/ML Foundations for Sequential Imperfect-Information Strategy Games**

The pursuit of superhuman artificial intelligence in sequential, imperfect-information environments represents one of the most mathematically complex frontiers in machine learning. Unlike perfect-information environments such as Chess or Go, where the entire game state is fully observable and deterministic search algorithms like Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) can systematically converge on optimal play, games involving hidden hands, shuffled deck orders, and variable action spaces introduce severe partial observability. In these domains, the true state of the game is unknown to the acting agent. Attempting to apply standard Deep Q-Networks (DQN) or unaugmented Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to these environments routinely results in catastrophic failure. The literature clearly documents that naive reinforcement learning agents operating in partially observable, zero-sum scenarios succumb to non-transitive, cyclic unlearning, often oscillating endlessly between sub-optimal strategies without ever approaching a stable Nash equilibrium. To construct an expert-level artificial intelligence for a multi-round, resource-passing collectible card game, the architectural design must rigidly adhere to the foundational principles of imperfect-information game theory, regret minimization, belief-state modeling, and mathematically validated action masking.

This comprehensive analysis synthesizes the primary source literature surrounding imperfect-information game artificial intelligence, extracting precise methodologies for heuristic search, self-play planner design, neural network observation encoding, evaluation infrastructures, and controllable difficulty scaling. The structural realities of the target engine—featuring a deterministic core, a hidden-information-safe legal-move contract, sparse terminal rewards, and complex round-passing mechanics—dictate that any proposed algorithm must explicitly demonstrate efficacy in partially observable domains without relying on hidden-state cheating or flat determinization.

## **Legal Heuristic V1 Design and Move Pruning Architectures**

The transition from a deliberately weak deterministic policy to a robust baseline heuristic requires immediate structural enhancements to navigate the combinatorial branching factor inherent to collectible card games. The Legends of Code and Magic (LOCM) AI competition provides an exact environmental proxy, demonstrating that the sheer volume of legal actions in a card game renders exhaustive tree search computationally intractable within standard operational time limits.1 The primary architectural requirement for the legal-heuristic-v1 specification is the implementation of aggressive, mathematically sound move pruning.

In complex turn-based card games, an agent's legal action space frequently encompasses playing multiple cards, triggering specific leader abilities, selecting optimal targets across different rows, and responding to engine prompts. To optimize the heuristic search, the engine must incorporate strict lethal move detection.1 Lethal move detection functions as a terminal algorithmic override; if the engine calculates that a specific sequence of actions guarantees an immediate round win or forces an unrecoverable card deficit on the opponent, it permanently masks all other non-lethal action trajectories, reducing the branching factor for that specific node to exactly one.1

Beyond lethal detection, the heuristic must actively prune sub-optimal legal moves based on a static evaluation function. The literature indicates that evaluating board states through distinct aggressive and balanced heuristic parameters proves highly effective for managing the multi-round nature of card games.1 For the specific mechanic of round-passing—a critical strategic resource—the heuristic evaluation must continuously weigh the agent's current point advantage against the projected card expenditure required by the opponent to overcome that deficit. If the agent maintains a commanding point lead and the heuristic calculates that the opponent must expend multiple high-value resources to win the round, the heuristic should aggressively bias the probability distribution toward the pass action. This rule-based framework operates dualistically: it serves as a formidable, unexploitable baseline for future neural network evaluation, and it functions as an exceptionally fast, high-quality rollout policy for early-stage search algorithms.

## **Search and Self-Play Planner Convergence**

The selection of a search and self-play planner represents the most critical architectural decision for an imperfect-information engine. The historical reliance on Perfect Information Monte Carlo (PIMC), commonly known as determinization, has been proven systematically flawed for environments requiring strategic obfuscation.2 PIMC operates by sampling possible hidden states (such as dealing random plausible hands to the opponent), searching these hypothetical realities as perfect-information games, and averaging the expected utilities.2 While computationally straightforward, Cowling et al. formally demonstrate that PIMC suffers from a fatal theoretical vulnerability known as "strategy fusion".2

Strategy fusion occurs when the search algorithm erroneously assumes that an agent can take different, optimal actions in different determinizations of the exact same information set.2 In the context of a card game, an agent using PIMC might simulate a rollout where the opponent holds a board-clearing spell, and optimally choose to pass. In the next simulation, where the opponent holds weak units, the agent optimally chooses to play a high-value card. PIMC averages these utilities and assumes both outcomes are achievable. In reality, the agent cannot distinguish between these two states because the opponent's hand is hidden; it must choose a single action that performs robustly across the entire probability distribution of the information set.2 PIMC hallucinates clairvoyance, leading to catastrophic misplays, particularly regarding the timing of the round pass.

To rectify this, the planner must utilize Information Set Monte Carlo Tree Search (ISMCTS). ISMCTS structurally eliminates strategy fusion by building a search tree composed entirely of information sets rather than explicit, determinized states.2 When a node is expanded in ISMCTS, the statistics collected for a given action apply to the entire equivalence class of indistinguishable states.2 This forces the algorithm to evaluate the expected utility of an action against the full spectrum of uncertainty, naturally generating mixed policies that are mandatory for optimal play in zero-sum bluffing and resource-passing games.2 Furthermore, ISMCTS operates with superior computational efficiency in card games because it consolidates search statistics into a single unified tree, rather than fracturing its computational budget across dozens of separate perfect-information simulations.2

While ISMCTS serves as the optimal search planner, the self-play reinforcement learning loop requires equal rigor to prevent policy collapse. Standard proximal policy optimization is insufficient. The engine must implement a regret-based or average-policy framework to anchor the agent. Neural Fictitious Self-Play (NFSP) provides the most empirically validated, accessible architecture for this purpose.4 NFSP mitigates cyclic drift by utilizing a dual-network approach. The reinforcement learning network continuously attempts to find the best response to the opponent's current strategy, while the Supervised Learning network trains on a reservoir-sampled buffer of the agent's own historical best responses to compute a highly stable average strategy.4 This mechanism ensures that the agent slowly converges toward an approximate Nash equilibrium without infinitely cycling through counter-strategies when encountering non-transitive deck archetypes.4

If the engine's computational budget permits, Deep Counterfactual Regret Minimization (Deep CFR) offers an even more rigorous mathematical guarantee of convergence for extensive-form episodic games.6 By training an Advantage Network to predict counterfactual regrets across all infosets, Deep CFR operates natively on the principle of regret matching, selecting actions in direct proportion to their positive regret.6 A vital constraint when deploying Deep CFR or NFSP is the strict requirement for reservoir sampling within the memory buffers. The literature explicitly warns that sliding-window buffers cause catastrophic forgetting, as the neural network will discard foundational defensive strategies once they age out of the buffer, leaving the agent highly exploitable to older, previously defeated tactics.4

## **Mathematical Validation of ML Observation, Action, and Reward Design**

Translating the complex ruleset of a collectible card game into a tensor format suitable for deep neural networks requires meticulous structural engineering. The engine's provision of a hidden-information-safe per-row legal-action export is an immense asset, but it introduces the challenge of variable action spaces. Depending on the phase of the turn, the agent may have three legal mulligan choices, fifteen legal card plays, or merely the binary option to pass or use a leader ability.

Historically, practitioners handled variable action spaces either by dynamically resizing the output tensor—which shatters standardized batch processing and GPU optimization—or by penalizing the agent with heavy negative rewards whenever it sampled an invalid action.7 The literature decisively proves that negative reward penalization is computationally wasteful, severely destabilizes the policy gradient, and forces the network to expend millions of parameters merely learning the rules of the game rather than learning strategic mastery.

The mathematically correct architecture relies on Invalid Action Masking.7 Huang and Ontañón (2022) provide the definitive proof that invalid action masking does not violate the underlying mechanics of the policy gradient theorem.7 The process involves defining the action space as a static, maximally sized categorical distribution encompassing every theoretically possible action in the game.7 Before the final softmax activation layer, the engine provides a binary mask vector representing the strictly legal moves for that exact infoset. The logits corresponding to invalid actions are heavily penalized, typically set to a massive negative scalar (e.g., ![][image1]).7

Huang and Ontañón demonstrate that this masking operation functions as a state-dependent differentiable function.7 Consequently, the gradient corresponding to the masked logits becomes exactly zero, effectively halting any parameter updates for illegal trajectories while preserving a perfectly valid, normalized policy gradient for the legal actions.7 This proof guarantees that the engine's legal-move contract can be seamlessly hardcoded into the final layer of the policy network, drastically accelerating sample efficiency and mathematically constraining the agent to explore only the topology of legal play.

However, a flat categorical output vector becomes intractable when actions require complex targeting. If a card can be played on three different rows, and can target any of the opponent's ten active units, the combinatorics of a flattened action space explode.11 To resolve this, the network must adopt the autoregressive action embeddings pioneered by AlphaStar.13 Instead of a single massive output layer, the action head must factorize sequentially. The network first outputs a primary probability distribution for the base action type (e.g., "Play Unit," "Deploy Spell," "Pass"). The internal latent representation of this selected primary action is then fed forward as a conditional input to subsequent linear heads, which determine the target row and the target unit.12 This autoregressive structure elegantly handles conditional legal masking, as the secondary heads are only activated and masked based on the specific requirements of the primary action choice.

Reward design within this architecture must remain fiercely sparse and strictly terminal. Providing intermediate, dense rewards—such as granting positive scalars for dealing damage, boosting unit power, or drawing cards—inevitably introduces fatal human bias. Reinforcement learning agents are notorious for reward hacking; if rewarded for dealing damage, the agent may perpetually delay winning the game to maximize its damage output, leading to highly exploitable, pathological behavior. The reward signal must be restricted to a binary win/loss outcome at the conclusion of the match, or at most, a discrete reward distributed at the end of each of the three rounds to anchor the temporal credit assignment. The deep reinforcement learning algorithms (NFSP, Deep CFR, R-NaD) are explicitly designed to backpropagate these sparse terminal rewards across long time horizons to discover the true expected value of early-game actions.6

## **Non-Transitive Dynamics and Evaluation Ladder Methodologies**

Establishing that an artificial intelligence has genuinely improved in a collectible card game is uniquely difficult. In perfect-information games like Chess, skill is generally transitive; if Agent A defeats Agent B, and Agent B defeats Agent C, it is a mathematical certainty that Agent A will defeat Agent C.16 In these transitive environments, tracking a single, global Elo rating provides a highly accurate reflection of algorithmic strength.17

Collectible card games, however, are fundamentally non-transitive. The existence of distinct deck archetypes—such as aggressive swarm decks, slow engine-building decks, and reactive control decks—creates inescapable rock-paper-scissors dynamics.1 A newly trained agent might discover a brilliant strategy that achieves a 90% win rate against a control deck, but simultaneously unlearn its basic defensive posture, causing its win rate against aggro decks to plummet to 10%. If evaluated purely on an aggregated self-play win rate, this catastrophic regression might appear as a statistical plateau, masking the agent's structural collapse.

The literature mandates the implementation of a rigorous, multidimensional evaluation ladder to detect and quantify these non-transitive shifts. Standard Elo is insufficient because it assumes static variance in player skill.18 The evaluation harness must utilize advanced rating systems like Glicko-2, which explicitly calculate both rating deviation and rating volatility.16 Rating deviation measures the statistical confidence the system has in the agent's current rating, which naturally widens when an agent has not played specific matchups recently.18 Rating volatility tracks the expected magnitude of fluctuation in the agent's performance.18 A sudden spike in an agent's Glicko-2 volatility metric serves as an immediate, algorithmic warning sign that the policy network is experiencing catastrophic forgetting or cyclic unlearning, prompting intervention before the training run is ruined.

To operationalize this, the engine cannot rely on fluid self-play evaluation alone. It must construct a rigid Matchup Matrix. This matrix requires freezing previous iterations of the agent, alongside rule-based baseline heuristics, into a fixed-seed suite of opponents.1 The suite must represent the full spectrum of deck archetypes. During evaluation, the active training agent plays a statistically significant number of matches against every fixed opponent across identical deterministic seeds to eliminate draw variance. Progress is mathematically verified only when the agent's expected value increases across the entire matrix without degrading below a critical threshold in any specific archetype matchup.1

Furthermore, to mathematically prove that the self-play algorithm is converging toward a Nash equilibrium, the project should periodically compute approximate exploitability.19 Exploitability measures exactly how much expected value a static policy loses when facing its theoretical worst-case opponent (a mathematically calculated best response).6 While calculating exact exploitability is computationally impossible for large card games, approximate exploitability—derived by training a secondary RL agent exclusively to exploit the frozen primary agent—provides a vital proxy metric to ensure the primary policy is actually becoming robust, rather than just learning to defeat its own specific quirks.19

| Evaluation Metric | Mathematical Focus | Primary Function in CCG AI | Detection Capability |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Global Win Rate | Aggregate binary success. | High-level summary (often misleading). | Useless for non-transitive cycles. |
| Matchup Matrix | Archetype-specific expected value. | Isolates weakness against specific decks. | Identifies rock-paper-scissors regressions. |
| Glicko-2 Volatility | Expected fluctuation in skill rating. | Measures stability of the learning process. | Flags catastrophic forgetting or meta-shifts. |
| Approximate Exploitability | Distance from Nash Equilibrium. | Proves theoretical robustness of the policy. | Detects overfitting to self-play opponents. |

## **Bounded Rationality and Controllable Difficulty Scaling**

If the ultimate objective of the artificial intelligence includes interacting with human players, the engine must support controllable difficulty scaling. A pervasive and heavily criticized anti-pattern in game AI development is the reliance on "intentional blunders" to reduce difficulty.21 In this flawed paradigm, a grandmaster-level engine plays optimally 80% of the time, but forces a randomly selected, mathematically terrible move 20% of the time.21 The literature overwhelmingly confirms that human players immediately detect this pathological, "drunk" behavior, which shatters psychological immersion and creates highly frustrating user experiences.21 Humans do not play perfectly and then suddenly play randomly; humans execute localized, coherent plans but fail to calculate long-term probabilistic consequences.

To create genuinely human-like play at lower difficulty tiers, the engine must implement bounded rationality.23 This concept dictates that the agent should always attempt to play the best move it can find, but its capacity to find that move is structurally restricted by the engine. Bounded rationality can be engineered through two distinct methodologies depending on the underlying planner.

If the engine relies on Information Set Monte Carlo Tree Search (ISMCTS), difficulty scaling is achieved through strict Search Budgeting.3 By artificially throttling the maximum number of iterations or severely limiting the maximum expansion depth of the search tree, the algorithm is forced to evaluate the board state based purely on shallow, immediate tactics rather than deep strategic foresight.3 A low-difficulty ISMCTS agent will correctly identify immediate threats and execute localized synergies, but it will fail to accurately predict the opponent's responses two turns ahead, mimicking the cognitive limitations of a novice human player.3

If the engine relies on a neural network policy (such as the output of NFSP or Deep CFR), difficulty is controlled via Temperature Scaling applied to the output logits.24 By flattening the softmax probability distribution slightly, the agent is mathematically encouraged to select sub-optimal, but still highly ranked, actions with greater frequency. This produces a policy that consistently plays "good" cards but frequently misses the absolute "optimal" sequence, resulting in organic, believable mistakes that scale linearly with the applied temperature coefficient, entirely avoiding the catastrophic randomness of injected blunders.

## **Deck-Conditioned Generalization and League Play Infrastructure**

The final architectural consideration dictates the scope of the agent: whether the policy should be a faction-specialist (trained exclusively to pilot one specific deck) or deck-conditioned (capable of generalizing its strategy across any given deck composition). Faction specialists are computationally cheaper to train, as the observation space does not need to heavily encode the intricate synergies of the agent's own deck; the network implicitly memorizes the optimal sequences for that specific card list. However, maintaining a fleet of dozens of distinct specialist models creates an unsustainable inference architecture.

A deck-conditioned agent requires the observation tensor to explicitly embed the semantic properties, synergy flags, and resource costs of the deck it is currently piloting.25 When utilizing a deck-conditioned policy, the risk of catastrophic collapse during self-play increases exponentially. If the self-play matchmaking randomly pairs decks, the neural network will inevitably gravitate toward mastering whichever deck currently dominates the local meta, completely unlearning how to pilot weaker decks or how to defend against niche strategies.13

To successfully train a generalized, deck-conditioned agent, the project must implement the League Play methodology pioneered by AlphaStar.13 League Play fundamentally restructures the reinforcement learning pipeline. Instead of a single agent playing against its own most recent weights, the system maintains a diverse ecosystem of agents.26 The "Main Agent" trains continuously to maximize its win rate against the entire league.26 Crucially, the system periodically branches off "Main Exploiters," whose sole mathematical objective is to find fatal flaws in the Main Agent's current policy by utilizing highly specific, counter-meta deck archetypes.26 Additionally, "League Exploiters" are trained to find blind spots across the entire historical distribution of all agents.26

By forcing the Main Agent to constantly defend against these targeted, highly specialized exploiters, the League Play infrastructure mathematically guarantees that the network cannot collapse into a narrow, localized optimum.13 While this infrastructure requires substantial engineering to track the Elo matrices of the various exploiting branches, it is the only theoretically sound method to produce a single, unified neural network capable of piloting varying deck archetypes with superhuman, unexploitable proficiency in an imperfect-information collectible card game.
