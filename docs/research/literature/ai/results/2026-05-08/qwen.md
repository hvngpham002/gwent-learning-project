# An Implementation Blueprint for Gwent AI: A Survey of Feasible Methods for Small-Scale Imperfect-Information Games

## Foundational Algorithms: Regret Minimization vs. Stochastic Search

The landscape of artificial intelligence for two-player zero-sum imperfect-information games is dominated by two primary algorithmic families: counterfactual regret minimization (CFR) and stochastic search methods, most notably Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) and its imperfect-information variant, Information Set MCTS (ISMCTS). Understanding the theoretical underpinnings, computational demands, and practical applicability of each is fundamental to making informed decisions for a Gwent-style AI project operating under small academic compute constraints. While both families aim to find approximate Nash equilibria, their methodologies and feasibility profiles differ significantly.

Counterfactual Regret Minimization (CFR) stands as one of the most successful and theoretically grounded approaches for solving extensive-form games [[37](https://openreview.net/forum?id=ByebT3EYvr), [38](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~waugh/publications/regress15.pdf)]. The core principle of CFR involves iteratively traversing the game tree and updating strategy choices based on the "regret" of not having chosen other actions in past iterations [[39](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/30438383)]. Regret is calculated as the difference in utility between the action taken and a hypothetical alternative action, assuming all other players stick to their current strategies. Over many iterations, the average strategy across all iterations converges towards a solution that is arbitrarily close to a Nash equilibrium [[21](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373738083_A_Survey_of_Solving_Imperfect_Information_Game_and_their_Applications_in_Defensive_Deception)]. Landmark papers such as Brown and Sandholm's introduction of CFR+ demonstrated its power by enabling the first computer-professional human competition in heads-up limit Texas Hold'em poker [[28](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adg3256)]. Subsequent variants like Deep CFR further enhanced its capabilities by using deep neural networks to approximate the value function, allowing for scaling to larger games [[36](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.04344v1), [37](https://openreview.net/forum?id=ByebT3EYvr)]. These methods are often cited as the "large-scale equilibrium-finding algorithm of choice" for such problems [[38](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~waugh/publications/regress15.pdf)]. However, their strength comes at a significant computational cost. The iterative nature of CFR, which requires full traversals of the game tree in every iteration, makes it notoriously slow to train and highly demanding on memory and parallel processing resources [[128](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.06559), [131](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-022-00463-x)]. Even with modern GPU acceleration, solving moderately sized games can require weeks of computation on large clusters [[18](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950705123003179)].

In contrast, ISMCTS offers a different paradigm. Instead of explicitly calculating regrets over the entire game tree, ISMCTS performs a limited number of simulations (rollouts) from the current game state, treating the problem as a planning task rather than a full equilibrium computation [[5](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18242v2)]. It directly addresses imperfect information by creating multiple "determinizations" of the current information set—each representing a plausible guess about the hidden components of the game state, such as an opponent's hand [[67](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/download/12679/12527)]. Traditional MCTS is then run independently on each of these determinized game states. The results from all simulations are aggregated and averaged to make a final decision, effectively marginalizing out the uncertainty of the hidden information [[4](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0004370214001052)]. This approach is computationally much lighter than CFR because it only explores a small fraction of the game tree, guided by the upper confidence bound applied to trees (UCT) formula. Its suitability for real-time applications and single-machine environments makes it a prime candidate for projects with modest compute budgets. The foundational paper by Cowling, Powley, and Whitehouse on ISMCTS established its viability for games with incomplete information [[5](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18242v2)], and subsequent work has refined the technique, for instance by introducing re-determinizing to prevent information leakage between simulations [[118](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.06075)].

For the specified Gwent AI project, this distinction is critical. High-resource systems like Pluribus, Cicero, and Libratus provide invaluable conceptual background on how to tackle complex imperfect-information games [[3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.09451), [96](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.04714)]. Pluribus, for example, combined self-play reinforcement learning with search, demonstrating a powerful hybrid approach [[96](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.04714)]. However, these systems were trained on vast computational infrastructure, far exceeding the resources available to a small academic team [[96](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.04714)]. Therefore, while their existence validates the overall research direction, they should be considered background-only references rather than direct implementation targets. The primary question becomes whether a more scalable method like ISMCTS can achieve a comparable level of performance relative to the project's specific engine and constraints.

Its lower implementation risk and smaller resource requirements make it a pragmatic choice. ## ISMCTS and Hybrid Learning for Card Games

Information Set Monte Carlo Tree Search (ISMCTS) emerges from the literature as the most technically relevant and computationally feasible foundation for developing a Gwent-style AI. Its architecture is inherently suited to the characteristics of sequential, imperfect-information card games, offering a balance between strategic depth and practical implementation. The core innovation of ISMCTS lies in its ability to manage hidden information through a process called determinization [[67](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/download/12679/12527)]. When faced with an information set—a collection of game states indistinguishable to a player due to hidden information—the algorithm generates multiple "determinizations," each positing a specific, complete state by filling in the unknown variables, such as the opponent's hand [[67](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/download/12679/12527), [83](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.06066)]. Each of these determinized worlds is treated as a separate, fully observable game, and a standard MCTS is executed on each one. The outcomes of these individual searches are then aggregated to produce a single, informed decision for the root node [[4](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0004370214001052)]. This approach elegantly sidesteps the need for a complex belief-state representation or particle filter, which can be computationally intensive, while still accounting for uncertainty in the opponent's holdings.

This methodology aligns perfectly with the structural elements of Gwent. The game's turn-based, sequential nature maps directly onto the tree structure of an extensive-form game, which ISMCTS is designed to navigate [[2](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364072795_Learning_to_play_an_imperfect_information_card_game_using_reinforcement_learning)]. Furthermore, the prevalence of imperfect-information card games in the ISMCTS literature provides ample precedent. Research has successfully applied ISMCTS to games like DouDiZhu, another complex Chinese card game, demonstrating its effectiveness in domains with hidden hands and trick-taking mechanics [[7](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.11642?), [113](https://www.mdpi.com/2078-2489/11/3/141), [125](https://www.denexa.com/blog/dou-dizhu/?srsltid=AfmBOoqHsFI6azuMThED_TCEXJVUdDfLfzNap52iZu1LKqlBoaeMg1jq)]. This body of work suggests that ISMCTS is not merely a theoretical concept but a proven tool for building competitive agents in structurally similar environments. The algorithm's reliance on playouts and rollouts also makes it a natural fit for the project's existing engine, which supports deterministic headless AI-vs-AI simulation [[50](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08462v1)]. This compatibility allows for rapid development and evaluation without requiring fundamental changes to the underlying game loop.

However, a pure ISMCTS agent, relying solely on random playouts to evaluate leaf nodes, would likely perform poorly. Such an agent lacks strategic foresight and would struggle to differentiate between nuanced positions. To achieve a competitive level of play, especially against human opponents, ISMCTS must be hybridized with a learned evaluation function, often referred to as a "value network" or policy network [[1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.09451), [17](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05048v1)]. This learned component guides the search by providing a more accurate estimate of the value of a position, reducing the reliance on chance during playouts. Several sources highlight this synergy; for instance, work on LAMIR introduces an algorithm that learns an abstracted model of the game directly from experience to improve look-ahead reasoning, a concept directly transferable to enhancing ISMCTS [[17](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.05048v1)]. Similarly, the application of ISMCTS in games like Avalon, where players gain new private information over time, underscores the importance of integrating learned models to capture evolving strategic dynamics [[22](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.02330)]. For the Gwent project, this means that after selecting ISMCTS as the planner, the next logical step is to develop a machine learning model whose purpose is to predict the likelihood of winning from a given game state.

The integration of a learned model into ISMCTS creates a powerful feedback loop. The agent uses the policy/value network to guide its search, and the results of those searches (i.e., the outcomes of games played via self-play) can be used to further train and refine the network. This hybrid approach combines the exhaustive exploration capabilities of search with the pattern-recognition strengths of deep learning. The literature on Hearthstone provides a compelling case study for this methodology. One successful agent used an end-to-end policy function, trained with Optimistic Smooth Fictitious Play (OSFP), to guide its search [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)]. This demonstrates that self-play can be a viable training paradigm for imperfect-information CCGs, producing agents that compete effectively against both weaker heuristics and even "cheat" versions with full state visibility [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)]. The success of this approach provides strong evidence that a similar ISMCTS-plus-learning framework is a viable and promising path forward for the Gwent AI project. The implementation risk is therefore low, as the components are well-understood and their combination has a track record of success in analogous domains.

## Action Representation and Policy Learning in Variable Environments

A defining characteristic of card games like Gwent is their dynamic action space; the set of legal moves available to a player changes from turn to turn depending on their hand, the battlefield state, and the current phase of the game (e.g., mulligan, main play, prompt response) [[27](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/12/11/2453)]. Effectively managing this variability is a critical engineering challenge that directly impacts an AI's ability to learn and perform. The literature provides several robust techniques for handling such structured, variable-size action spaces, with action masking and action decomposition emerging as the most prominent and effective strategies. These methods allow neural networks to operate on a fixed-size output layer while accommodating a sparse and changing set of valid actions, ensuring the agent never attempts an illegal move.

Action masking is a straightforward yet powerful technique where, at each decision point, a binary mask is generated to indicate which actions in the agent's full action space are legal [[23](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04542v1), [24](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.27751v1)]. This mask is then applied to the output of the neural network's final softmax layer before the action is selected. Any action deemed illegal by the mask will have its probability set to zero, forcing the selection algorithm to choose from the remaining valid options [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197), [25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.27751)]. This approach is computationally efficient and integrates seamlessly into standard deep reinforcement learning pipelines. It ensures that the agent's policy is always conditioned on the legal moves available, preventing invalid actions from corrupting the learning process. The successful application of this technique in the context of the Hearthstone AI competition serves as a primary source validation, confirming its practical utility in a complex CCG environment [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)].

While action masking solves the problem of filtering illegal actions, it does not address the inefficiency of predicting among a very large, mostly empty action space. A more sophisticated approach, employed by the winning agent in the Legends of Code and Magic (LOCM) competition, is action decomposition [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)]. Instead of generating a single probability distribution over the entire action space, the model breaks the action selection into a sequence of smaller, conditional predictions. First, it predicts the "type" of action to take—for example, playing a specific card from the hand, using a hero power, or passing. Then, conditioned on the selected type, it predicts the "target" of that action, such as which enemy character to attack or which row to place a card on. This hierarchical, auto-regressive structure dramatically reduces the complexity of the prediction task at each stage [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)]. For instance, instead of choosing one index from a vector of hundreds of possible moves, the network might first choose one of five types of actions and then choose one of ten possible targets, a far more manageable learning problem. This modular design is highly scalable and naturally accommodates the diverse actions found in Gwent.

Building upon these representation techniques, the design of the observation and reward signals is equally crucial for guiding effective policy learning. The input to the neural network, or the "observation," must be a compact yet informative summary of the game state from the agent's perspective. Since the engine already produces a per-seat safe observation, the focus should be on feature engineering to extract meaningful information. This observation vector should include features for cards in hand, the status of each battlefield row, the composition of the remaining deck, the current round score, and the identity of the active player. Crucially, it must not leak information about the opponent's private state. The output of the network, as discussed, should be structured according to the action decomposition scheme. For the reward signal, the project's existing setup of sparse terminal rewards is a good starting point [[50](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08462v1)]. These rewards can be binary (+1 for a win, 0 for a loss, or -1 for a loss) and backpropagated through the search tree to update the value estimates and, subsequently, the policy network weights. By combining a carefully engineered observation space, an action-decomposed policy network with masked outputs, and a simple terminal reward structure, the project can construct a powerful and tractable machine learning pipeline capable of driving a sophisticated ISMCTS agent.

## Evaluation Frameworks for Deterministic Headless Simulation

Establishing a rigorous and reproducible evaluation framework is paramount for tracking progress, validating improvements, and comparing different AI agents in the absence of live human players. Given the project's existing support for a deterministic, headless AI-vs-AI simulation engine with fixed seeds, the ideal evaluation methodology should leverage these capabilities to the fullest. The literature provides a suite of established metrics and protocols, including Elo rating systems, Glicko ratings, matchup matrices, and exploitability proxies, which are all compatible with automated batch testing. These tools allow a small academic team to build a robust "evaluation ladder" that provides quantitative feedback on an agent's strength and strategic diversity.

One of the most accessible and widely understood metrics for ranking agent strength is the Elo rating system, originally developed for chess [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)]. In this framework, each AI agent is assigned a numerical rating. After a series of matches against other agents, their ratings are updated based on the outcome of those games. If an agent with a higher rating wins, it gains fewer points than if it had lost, providing a dynamic measure of performance relative to its perceived strength. A more advanced version, Glicko, adds a "ratings reliability" (RD) component, which accounts for the uncertainty in an agent's true skill level, making it more responsive to recent performance changes [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)]. Both systems are straightforward to implement and provide a clear, quantifiable way to track an agent's improvement over time through successive training iterations. The LOCM competition paper notes the use of Elo/Glicko-style evaluations in their tournament harnesses, confirming its relevance for CCG AI [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)].

While Elo and Glicko provide a global ranking, matchup matrices offer a more granular view of an agent's performance profile. A matchup matrix records the win rates of every agent in a population against every other agent [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)]. This reveals important strategic insights that a single rating might obscure. For example, an agent might have a mediocre overall Elo rating but excel against certain archetypes while being weak against others. This detailed breakdown is invaluable for identifying weaknesses in an agent's strategy and for curating a balanced set of benchmarks. For the Gwent project, a matchup matrix could compare the new ISMCTS agent against a baseline heuristic (`legal-heuristic-v0`), a few simpler search agents, and potentially different deck-conditioned specialists. This would provide a multi-faceted assessment of its capabilities.

Perhaps the most theoretically sound, though often difficult to compute, metric is exploitability. In a two-player zero-sum game, the exploitability of a strategy measures how much better an opponent could do by switching to a different strategy. A strategy with zero exploitability is a Nash equilibrium. While calculating the exact exploitability of a complex game like Gwent is intractable, it is possible to compute an *approximate* exploitability [[96](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.04714)]. This is typically done by measuring how much an agent's win rate improves when it is allowed to best-respond to a fixed opponent strategy. Lower exploitability indicates a more robust and less predictable agent. Some research highlights the pursuit of unexploitable, machine-perfect play as a key goal, underscoring the importance of this metric [[96](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.04714)]. Although implementing a full exploitability calculation may be beyond the scope of a near-term project, understanding this concept informs the design of the evaluation ladder. A strong evaluation protocol should include regression baselines, where the new agent must consistently beat all previous versions, and anti-overfitting protocols, where the agent is tested against a mixture of historical checkpoints rather than a single static opponent [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)]. This prevents the agent from optimizing for a specific, non-evolving target and encourages the development of a genuinely stronger strategy. The combination of Elo ratings for trend analysis, matchup matrices for strategic profiling, and robust regression tests for performance assurance provides a comprehensive evaluation framework that is perfectly aligned with the project's technical constraints and goals.

## Modeling Strategic Mechanics: Rounds, Passing, and Deck Conditioning

To create a truly Gwent-like AI, the system must not only understand the rules of card play but also grasp the high-level strategic concepts that define the game's unique identity. Key mechanics such as the three-round match structure, the conservation of passing as a resource, and the influence of a pre-selected deck all contribute to the game's depth. The provided literature, while sparse on Gwent-specific models, offers valuable principles and techniques for encoding these strategic elements into an AI agent. Successfully modeling these aspects is crucial for moving beyond a rule-following bot to a strategic opponent that can adapt its playstyle across rounds and leverage its hand composition effectively.

The three-round structure of Gwent necessitates a form of long-term planning that balances short-term tactical wins with the ultimate goal of match victory. An agent cannot simply optimize for winning each round independently; it must consider the cumulative score and the potential for future rounds. Several approaches can be derived from the literature to achieve this. One effective method is to augment the agent's observation vector with contextual information about the current round and the running score [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)]. By feeding this information into the neural network, the policy can learn round-dependent behaviors implicitly. For example, the network might learn to play more conservatively in the first round to preserve key cards, take risks in the middle round to seize control, and adopt a defensive posture in the final round if behind. A more sophisticated approach involves hierarchical planning, where a high-level "macro" strategy model decides on a round-by-round objective (e.g., "aim to win the second round") that then guides the low-level ISMCTS planner [[32](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1609/aaai.v33i01.33011206)]. This separation of concerns allows for more deliberate strategic execution. Another powerful technique for creating diverse and strategically coherent opponents is deck-conditioned policies [[96](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.04714)]. Instead of training a single monolithic agent, the project could train multiple specialist agents, each conditioned on a specific deck or faction archetype. The input to the neural network would include an embedding representing the deck's identity, allowing the policy to learn distinct strategies tailored to that deck's strengths and weaknesses. This approach facilitates the creation of opponents with human-like styles and is a key enabler for difficulty scaling.

The act of passing in Gwent is a multifaceted strategic tool, serving as both a resource conservation mechanism and a potential bluff. It is not merely a fallback option but a legitimate action with informational value. An AI must be able to reason about when to pass. From a game-theoretic perspective, the decision to pass should be influenced by a holistic assessment of the game state: the current score differential, the perceived strength of one's own hand compared to the opponent's likely holdings, the cards that have already been played, and the composition of the remaining decks [[27](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/12/11/2453)]. The literature on imperfect-information games provides the necessary tools for this kind of reasoning. The ISMCTS framework naturally incorporates passing as a first-class action. During the search process, the agent will evaluate the expected value of passing versus playing any card in its hand. The learned policy network plays a critical role here by helping the agent assess the value of "waiting" and preserving its hand for a more opportune moment. The strategic depth of passing is analogous to pot control and bluffing in poker, concepts extensively studied in the context of imperfect-information solvers [[96](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.04714)]. While no single paper may be dedicated to "strategic passing in card games," the principles of hidden information and value-of-information are central to the field [[49](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07251v4)]. The agent's ability to model its opponent's likely hand and anticipate future board states is what enables it to make intelligent decisions about when to pass.

Finally, the choice between a generalist agent and a collection of specialist agents conditioned on deck or faction is a critical architectural decision. A generalist agent, trained to play any deck, offers simplicity and flexibility. However, it may struggle to master the nuances of highly specialized deck archetypes. A faction-balanced training mixture, where the agent is trained on a variety of deck types, can help mitigate this but may lead to a "jack of all trades, master of none" scenario [[96](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.04714)]. On the other hand, conditioning policies on a deck/faction representation allows for the creation of highly specialized agents that excel at specific strategies [[96](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.04714)]. This approach is supported by research in multi-agent systems and opponent modeling, where agents are tailored to specific roles or playstyles [[91](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/iel8/9739/5451756/11155147.pdf)]. For the Gwent project, a hybrid strategy seems most promising. The initial effort should focus on building a strong, generalist ISMCTS agent. Once this baseline is established, the team can begin training specialist agents conditioned on major factions or deck archetypes. This would allow for the construction of a diverse AI population, enabling more sophisticated difficulty scaling and creating a more engaging experience for human players. This phased approach mitigates risk while paving the way for more advanced, human-like opponents in the future.

## Implementation Blueprint and Strategic Recommendations

Synthesizing the findings from the reviewed literature, a clear and actionable blueprint emerges for the development of a competitive Gwent-style AI under small academic compute constraints. This plan prioritizes methods that are computationally feasible, technically sound, and directly applicable to the project's specific game engine and goals. The recommended approach is a hybrid system centered on Information Set Monte Carlo Tree Search (ISMCTS) augmented with a learned policy and value network, drawing heavily on proven techniques from the broader CCG AI community.

The foundational algorithm for the project should be **ISMCTS**. Its computational efficiency relative to regret-minimization algorithms like CFR makes it uniquely suited for a small-team development cycle [[2](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364072795_Learning_to_play_an_imperfect_information_card_game_using_reinforcement_learning), [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18242v2)]. The ISMCTS framework naturally handles Gwent's core challenges: imperfect information via determinization, variable legal action sets through perspective-based trees, and sequential decision-making within a multi-round structure [[4](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0004370214001052), [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18242v2)]. The initial implementation should focus on a stable baseline agent, which can be achieved by combining ISMCTS with a domain-specific heuristic for evaluating leaf nodes. This avoids the complexity of training a neural network upfront while establishing a functional and testable AI.

The next critical step is to integrate a **neural network policy/value function** to guide the ISMCTS search. This component is essential for achieving a level of play that can meaningfully compete with human opponents. The architecture of this network should be inspired by the successful agent developed for the Legends of Code and Magic (LOCM) competition [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)]. Specifically, the action selection process should employ **action decomposition**, breaking down the choice into a sequence of predictions (e.g., action type, then action target) to efficiently manage the game's variable action space. This must be paired with **action masking**, where a binary mask zeros out probabilities for illegal actions at each step, ensuring the agent remains compliant with the game's rules [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197), [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04542v1)]. The input to this network must be a thoughtfully engineered observation space that captures all relevant game state information from the agent's perspective without leaking hidden data, including hand contents, battlefield status, round scores, and deck sizes.

For training, the project should adopt a **self-play reinforcement learning framework**. The use of Optimistic Smooth Fictitious Play (OSFP) from the LOCM work is a strong candidate, as it has been shown to converge on the last-iterate policy, guaranteeing that the final checkpoint model is optimal without needing to maintain a separate average policy [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)]. The training protocol should involve the agent playing against a mixture of its own historical checkpoints, a method that promotes continuous improvement and helps prevent overfitting to a single opponent [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)]. This self-play loop will generate the data needed to iteratively refine the neural network's policy and value estimates, leading to increasingly sophisticated gameplay.

Evaluation must be an integral part of the development process, leveraging the project's deterministic headless simulation engine. A comprehensive **evaluation ladder** should be established, comprising:
1.  **Matchup Matrices:** To systematically compare the new agent's win rates against a set of established benchmarks, including `legal-heuristic-v0` and any previously trained agents. This will reveal strategic strengths and weaknesses [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)].
2.  **Elo Ratings:** To provide a quantitative, trend-based measure of the agent's overall performance improvement over successive training runs [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)].
3.  **Regression Baselines:** To ensure that each new model is demonstrably stronger than its predecessors, providing a clear signal of progress and preventing performance degradation [[6](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05197)].

As a long-term strategic goal, the project should explore **deck-conditioned policies** to create a diverse population of AI opponents [[96](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.04714)]. By training specialist agents for different factions or deck archetypes, the project can move toward creating human-like opponents with varied and identifiable playstyles. This will be a key enabler for implementing controllable difficulty and providing a more engaging and replayable experience for human players. By following this phased approach—from a baseline ISMCTS agent to a hybrid learning system, and finally to a population of specialized agents—the project can achieve significant milestones within its resource constraints while laying a solid foundation for future expansion.
